# Composition strategy

## Start from the material signature

Before drawing modules, write six short answers:

1. What single question should the poster answer?
2. What is the material's natural narrative: journey, diagnosis, comparison, architecture, evidence, campaign, or decision?
3. Which content is unique enough to become the dominant visual?
4. Which content is sequential, parallel, hierarchical, relational, or contrastive?
5. What can be removed without weakening the answer?
6. Where should the reader pause, decide, or act?

Use those answers to choose or evaluate the poster's silhouette. A proven template is a valid starting point when its structure suits these answers.

## Choose one narrative spine

| Material characteristic | Often suitable forms | Other forms to consider when needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear stages or maturity path | vertical journey, river, stepped field, milestone band | numbered cards, timeline, or modular process |
| Competing choices or before/after state | split comparison, mirrored field, decision fork | comparison cards, matrix, or staged contrast |
| Interacting actors, systems, or data | system map, orbit, layered architecture, central hub | relational cards or annotated flow |
| Several cases with distinct diagnoses | asymmetric case field, indexed atlas, dominant case plus satellites | equal diagnosis cards when cases carry equal weight |
| Strong evidence, examples, or proof | evidence stack, annotated exhibit, large proof panel | evidence cards or editorial sequence |
| One core proposition with supporting ideas | central statement, radial support, editorial feature layout | numbered sections or modular support cards |
| Governance or risk is the differentiator | boundary map, control gates, layered safeguards | a prominent governance module within a proven layout |

The table is a routing guide, not a layout library. Combine patterns only when their relationships are genuinely present in the source.

## Make structural decisions explicitly

- Set the dominant module to roughly 25–45% of the meaningful poster area when the source has one decisive idea.
- Use asymmetry when the material has unequal importance. Equal cards imply equal weight.
- Use section numbers only when sequence or navigation benefits from them. Do not number editorial or comparison layouts automatically.
- Let card count follow content count and the chosen layout's visual capacity.
- Alternate dense and quiet regions. Do not make every section a bordered card field.
- Change background, width, alignment, or visual scale at genuine narrative turns, not at fixed intervals.
- Use a process line only for actual temporal or operational sequence.
- Add material-specific visual treatment when it improves understanding; do not force novelty for its own sake.

## Evaluate template fit

Before final art generation, evaluate the chosen template or custom composition:

- Does its hierarchy make the main message easy to find?
- Does it accommodate the real number, length, and relationship of content items?
- Are any important ideas compressed, split, or overemphasized merely to fit the layout?
- Would a local adjustment solve the mismatch, or would another layout communicate better?

If the template fits well, retain it—including its module count, card count, and geometry. Similarity to an approved reference is not a defect. Redesign only when the current content, channel, or user preference calls for it.

## Keep style separate from layout

An approved reference may inform palette, line quality, icon family, typography, spacing, tone, narrative order, module count, card count, column ratio, scene placement, and total height. Decide which aspects to retain based on suitability rather than a blanket prohibition.
